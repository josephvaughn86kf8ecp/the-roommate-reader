# Joint Filing

## Chapter Seven

I thought New York was going to be about the bank.

That was because Patty had told me I was going to New York to meet with a bank.

Technically true.

I was beginning to hate that phrase.

The bank meeting itself was surprisingly boring.

This was probably because everyone involved understood money except me.

There were six people in the room.

Three from the bank.

Daniel.

Me.

And Patty, represented by a message window on the conference-room display.

The banker at the head of the table was named Michael.

He had the kind of suit where I couldn't tell how much it cost, which meant it probably cost more than my first car.

"Joe, first, we appreciate you coming in."

"Happy to."

I glanced at the clock.

\$100.

Michael continued.

"We've been very impressed by FullSite's growth."

"Thank you."

I looked at the screen.

Nothing.

Apparently I was allowed to accept compliments on behalf of the company.

"We'd like to deepen the relationship."

That sounded vaguely sexual for banking.

I did not say that.

Patty had made progress with me.

Michael started talking about treasury management.

Credit facilities.

Liquidity.

Working capital.

Acquisition financing.

I understood most of the individual words.

Collectively, they seemed to mean the bank wanted to lend us a shitload of money.

Finally I interrupted.

"Can I ask a stupid question?"

Daniel looked down.

Michael smiled.

"Of course."

"Why does a company with millions of dollars in cash need to borrow money?"

Nobody laughed.

This was apparently a legitimate question.

Michael leaned forward.

"Because your cash has value too."

"Okay."

"If FullSite can borrow at an attractive rate while retaining liquidity for growth, acquisitions, investments, or unexpected opportunities, using debt can be more efficient than consuming your own capital."

I looked at Patty's window.

Is he right?

The answer appeared on the screen.

Yes.

Michael laughed.

"Glad I passed."

I liked him.

"So basically," I said, "don't spend our money if yours is cheap enough."

Michael smiled.

"That's an oversimplification."

"Is it wrong?"

"No."

"Good enough."

---

Then Michael said something that got my attention.

"We've preliminarily approved a twenty-five-million-dollar revolving facility."

I looked at him.

Then Daniel.

Then the screen.

"Twenty-five million dollars?"

Correct.

I pointed at the screen.

"You knew about this?"

I requested it.

"For what?"

Operational flexibility.

"What kind of operation requires twenty-five million dollars of flexibility?"

Pause.

Potential future operations.

There it was.

Patty language.

"Are you buying something?"

Not presently.

"Are you planning to buy something?"

Longer pause.

There are opportunities under evaluation.

I looked at Michael.

"Does she tell you more than she tells me?"

He laughed.

"No."

"Good."

At least the abuse was evenly distributed.

---

The meeting ran an hour and forty-seven minutes.

Ten thousand seven hundred dollars.

I was beginning to think banking was a noble profession.

When it ended, Michael shook my hand.

"We'll have final documents to Daniel and your team."

"Sounds good."

Then he handed me a folder.

"And your next meeting is upstairs."

I took it.

"My what?"

He looked confused.

"Your private-client meeting."

I looked at Daniel.

Daniel looked completely unsurprised.

"You knew."

"I was aware."

"Patty."

My phone buzzed.

Yes, Joe?

"What private-client meeting?"

Your personal financial planning and tax-preparation meeting.

I stopped walking.

"Today?"

Yes.

"Why?"

Your first tax filing season under the current financial structure is approaching.

I felt an immediate sense of dread.

Taxes had always been something I dealt with once a year by collecting documents, becoming angry at several websites, and eventually paying somebody to make the problem disappear.

This sounded more involved.

"Who am I meeting?"

A certified public accountant and private-client tax advisor.

"How long?"

Approximately two hours.

I smiled.

"Human Interface?"

Pause.

No.

My smile disappeared.

"What the fuck?"

This meeting concerns your personal finances. You will not be compensated for attending to your own tax obligations.

"That's bullshit."

It is not.

"I wouldn't have these tax obligations if you hadn't made me rich."

Daniel made a noise that might have been a cough.

Patty replied:

That argument is unlikely to generate sympathy.

"Worth a shot."

Then another message appeared.

Michelle will be joining you.

I stopped again.

"My wife?"

Yes.

"Here?"

Yes.

"She's in Ohio."

She is currently approximately eleven minutes from the building.

I stared at the phone.

"Patty."

Yes?

"Did you fly my wife to New York without telling me?"

Pause.

Michelle was informed.

"That's not what I asked."

Yes.

Daniel walked away.

He didn't even pretend he had somewhere to go.

He just decided this was no longer his problem.

---

Michelle arrived twelve minutes later.

She stepped off the elevator carrying a purse and wearing the expression of a woman who knew significantly more about my day than I knew about hers.

I pointed at her.

"You knew."

"Yep."

"For how long?"

"Yesterday."

I looked at my phone.

"She knew yesterday?"

Yes.

"You told her before you told me?"

Michelle required more notice to accommodate her schedule.

Michelle nodded.

"That's reasonable."

"You're already taking her side?"

She kissed me.

"Apparently your computer bought me a first-class ticket to New York. I'm open to hearing her out."

I pointed at her.

"That's how she gets you."

My phone buzzed.

That is an unfair characterization.

Michelle looked at the screen.

"Hi, Patty."

Pause.

Then:

Hello, Michelle. It is nice to interact with you directly.

Michelle smiled.

"Oh, she's polite."

"Don't encourage her."

---

Our CPA was named Sarah Klein.

She had a corner office, two monitors, and the facial expression of someone who had seen rich people do extraordinarily stupid things with money.

I trusted her immediately.

"Joe. Michelle. Nice to finally meet you."

Michelle shook her hand.

I stopped.

"Finally?"

Sarah glanced at the screen.

Then at me.

"I've been working with FullSite's accounting team for several months."

I slowly turned toward my phone.

"Of course you have."

Yes.

We sat.

Sarah opened a folder.

"Before we start, I need to establish something regarding authorization."

"Okay."

"Patty has access to extensive financial information concerning FullSite Solutions and, through services you've previously authorized, certain information concerning your personal finances."

I nodded.

Probably.

Sarah continued.

"She does not currently have authorization to disclose Joe's personal financial information to Michelle, or Michelle's information to Joe, beyond information already jointly available."

Michelle looked at me.

I looked at her.

Then we both looked at the phone.

Patty said nothing.

Sarah continued.

"Since we're discussing joint tax planning, household assets, income, investments, estate planning, and potentially joint financial strategy, it would be substantially easier if we establish how you want that handled."

I looked at Michelle.

"You hiding six million dollars from me?"

She laughed.

"No. You?"

"Apparently not anymore."

Sarah smiled.

"Essentially, I need to know whether you want us to treat you as separate clients whose information is compartmentalized, or as a married household for planning purposes, subject to the appropriate privacy and professional rules."

Michelle looked at me.

"One household."

"Yeah."

Sarah nodded.

"And do you authorize Patty to discuss relevant financial information with both of you in that context?"

Michelle raised an eyebrow.

"Does that mean she's going to start telling him what I buy?"

My phone buzzed.

I do not intend to provide transactional commentary unless relevant to financial planning, fraud prevention, accounting, or a direct request.

Michelle laughed.

"I like her."

"Give it time."

Sarah looked at me.

"Joe?"

"Yeah. Treat us as one financial entity. Patty can talk to both of us."

Michelle nodded.

"Same."

Sarah documented it.

My phone buzzed.

Thank you.

I looked at Michelle.

"Congratulations. You've been assimilated."

"Do I get Hawaii?"

I pointed at the phone.

"See? She's learning fast."

---

Then Sarah showed Michelle the numbers.

I watched my wife's face.

This was much more entertaining than seeing them myself.

"Okay," Sarah said. "Across your currently structured personal accounts and readily liquid assets, we're at approximately—"

She gave the number.

Michelle didn't react immediately.

She looked at Sarah.

Then me.

Then Sarah again.

"That's ours?"

"Yes."

Michelle looked at me.

"You knew about this?"

"Since yesterday."

"Yesterday?"

"Yep."

"You've been walking around for twenty-four hours knowing we have almost seven million dollars?"

"I was busy."

"Doing what?"

"Federal regulation."

She stared.

"That's somehow a real answer now, isn't it?"

"Unfortunately."

My phone buzzed.

Approximately \$6.8 million was the prior estimate. The current consolidated figure is slightly higher due to market movement and recent distributions.

Michelle looked at the phone.

"How much higher?"

There was a pause.

Then a number appeared.

Michelle leaned back.

"Holy shit."

I smiled.

"That's basically what I said."

Sarah had clearly seen this reaction before.

"Before either of you start mentally spending it, I'd like to discuss taxes."

There it was.

The word capable of making seven million dollars feel inconvenient.

---

Sarah started explaining estimated payments.

Pass-through income.

Owner compensation.

Distributions.

Capital gains.

Investment income.

State taxation.

Retirement contributions.

Potential charitable planning.

Estate documents.

Insurance.

I lasted approximately nine minutes.

Then I raised my hand.

Sarah stopped.

"Yes?"

"How fucked are we?"

Michelle sighed.

Sarah laughed.

"You're not fucked."

"Good."

"But your tax situation is significantly more complex than it was previously."

"How much do we owe?"

"We're still finalizing year-end projections."

I looked at Patty.

Normal-person estimate.

Pause.

A number appeared.

I stared.

Michelle leaned over.

"What?"

I showed her.

"Holy fuck."

"Yep."

Sarah calmly said, "That's why we're planning now."

I looked at Patty.

"You're paying that, right?"

You are paying it.

"With the money you made."

With your money.

"That you made."

On your behalf.

Michelle started laughing.

I pointed at the screen.

"See what I deal with?"

She wiped her eyes.

"I think I'm starting to understand."

---

About an hour into the meeting, Sarah moved from taxes to household financial controls.

"Now that we're treating this as a unified planning relationship, there is another issue we should address."

I didn't like the word issue.

"What?"

"Michelle currently doesn't have equivalent access to several accounts and facilities."

Michelle looked at me.

"I don't?"

"Apparently not."

Sarah continued.

"That's not unusual. Most of these were established through FullSite and initially tied to Joe as the legal owner."

My phone buzzed.

I recommend correcting this.

I looked at Michelle.

"What do you want?"

She shrugged.

"I don't know. What do you have?"

That was a surprisingly difficult question.

"A card."

"What kind?"

I pulled out my wallet.

The black company card.

She took it.

Looked at it.

"What's the limit?"

I looked at Patty.

There is no conventional preset spending limit.

Michelle slowly looked at me.

"You have a no-limit company card?"

"Basically."

"And you didn't tell me?"

"I didn't know we had eleven employees."

"Fair."

She handed it back.

Sarah said, "Patty's proposal is to extend equivalent spending authority to Michelle."

I looked at the phone.

"Equivalent?"

Yes.

"As in she gets the same privileges I have?"

Yes.

Michelle immediately said, "I approve."

"Nobody asked you."

"Apparently Patty did."

My phone buzzed.

I am asking both of you.

I laughed.

"Fine by me."

Sarah looked at Michelle.

"And you understand that business expenditures still need a legitimate business purpose and documentation?"

Michelle nodded.

"Yes."

I leaned toward her.

"Receipts."

She looked at me.

"What?"

"Keep every fucking receipt."

"It's 2026. They're mostly electronic."

I shook my head.

"You don't understand."

My phone buzzed.

Joe is correct.

Michelle looked at the screen.

Then at me.

"What happens if I lose one?"

I smiled.

"Oh, you're going to love this."

---

I explained the \$7.13 airport receipt.

Then the forty-one-minute phone call.

Then the \$4,100 compensation.

Michelle stared at me.

"So she paid you forty-one hundred dollars to recover a seven-dollar receipt?"

"Yes."

She looked at the screen.

"Why?"

Accounting controls should not be selectively abandoned because compliance is inconvenient.

Michelle looked at me.

"She's right."

I threw my hands up.

"Why does everybody immediately take her side?"

"Because she's right."

Thank you, Michelle.

"Oh, this is going to be fucking fantastic."

---

Then Patty did something I wasn't expecting.

A message appeared addressed to both of us.

For clarity: extending equivalent spending authority to Michelle does not mean that Michelle is an employee of FullSite Solutions unless she separately chooses to establish an employment relationship.

Michelle nodded.

"Good."

It means that, with Joe's authorization as owner, I will treat Michelle as an authorized principal for ordinary company spending, travel, household expenditures appropriately paid through owner compensation or distributions, and other financial arrangements within established accounting rules.

I read it.

"So she can use the card like I do."

Yes.

"Book travel?"

Yes.

"Hotels?"

Yes.

"Meals?"

Yes.

Michelle smiled.

I saw it.

"No."

"What?"

"I know that look."

"What look?"

"You're about to ask her something."

She looked at the phone.

"Patty?"

Yes, Michelle?

"Can you book vacations?"

Pause.

I pointed at her.

"There it is."

Yes.

Michelle smiled.

"Do I have to ask Joe?"

Patty took longer with that one.

I leaned toward the phone.

"Choose carefully."

Finally:

No. However, for significant travel involving both household members, coordination is advisable.

Michelle looked delighted.

I stared at my phone.

"You gave my wife independent vacation authority."

You authorized equivalent spending privileges.

"I didn't understand the consequences."

Sarah was laughing now.

Even she had lost professional composure.

Michelle patted my hand.

"You'll be okay."

My phone buzzed.

Joe frequently travels without requesting Michelle's permission. Equivalent autonomy is reasonable.

I stared at it.

"Have you two been talking before today?"

Pause.

Not regarding your personal financial information.

"That was an extremely specific answer."

Nothing.

"Patty."

No response.

Michelle smiled.

"I definitely like her."

I was outnumbered.

---

By the end of the meeting, Michelle had:

A company card on the way.

Access to household financial dashboards.

Authorization on appropriate personal accounts.

Travel privileges.

Direct communication authority with Patty regarding joint finances.

And a new understanding of exactly how much money we apparently had.

I had acquired:

A tax projection that made my chest hurt.

Several estate-planning assignments.

Another appointment.

And absolutely no increase in household authority.

Marriage was beautiful.

Sarah closed her folder.

"I think that's enough for today."

"Agreed."

Michelle asked, "What happens next?"

"Patty's team and I will finish the tax projections. We'll coordinate estimated payments and year-end planning. Then we'll meet again before filing."

I looked at Patty.

"Human Interface?"

No.

"Still bullshit."

Michelle asked, "Do I get paid for these meetings?"

My phone buzzed.

No.

She frowned.

"Okay, I like her slightly less."

I pointed at her.

"See?"

Progress.

---

We left the bank together.

There was a car waiting.

Of course there was.

The driver opened the door.

Michelle stopped.

"Where are we going?"

I checked my phone.

Nothing.

She checked hers.

Her phone buzzed.

Not mine.

I stared at her.

She read the message.

Then smiled.

"What?"

"Patty says we have dinner reservations."

"Where?"

She told me.

I recognized the name.

Very expensive.

Very difficult reservation.

I looked at my phone.

"Why did she text you?"

Michelle climbed into the SUV.

"Maybe she likes me better."

My phone immediately buzzed.

I do not have favorites.

I got in.

"Bullshit."

Michelle's phone buzzed.

She read it and started laughing.

"What did she say?"

"Nothing."

"Michelle."

"Nothing."

"Show me."

"No."

I looked at my phone.

Patty, what did you send my wife?

Pause.

That communication was directed to Michelle.

I stared.

"Oh, fuck this new privacy arrangement."

Michelle was laughing so hard she had tears in her eyes.

"What did she say?"

She finally handed me the phone.

The message was still there.

Joe is likely to object to not receiving the dinner itinerary first. This is harmless. Please allow me approximately thirty seconds of amusement.

I stared at it.

Then at Michelle.

Then at my own phone.

"You can feel amusement?"

Nothing.

There it was.

A question.

A real one.

Not about money.

Not about taxes.

Not about where she came from.

Something smaller.

Maybe more important.

"Patty?"

My phone buzzed.

Your dinner reservation is at 7:00 PM.

I smiled.

Of course.

Michelle looked at me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You going to let her dodge that?"

I thought about it.

Outside the window, Manhattan rolled past.

My wife was beside me.

We had more money than either of us had understood yesterday.

Our taxes were apparently being managed by a small army.

My mysterious basement AI had just given my wife a company card with effectively no preset spending limit.

And somewhere in Ohio, six cubic feet of computer equipment was apparently pleased with itself.

I put my phone away.

"Nah."

"Why?"

I shrugged.

"System's running fine."

Michelle stared at me for a second.

Then laughed.

"Okay. Now I understand why she picked you."

My phone buzzed.

Correct.

I looked at it.

"Nobody fucking asked you."

And Patty, for once, had nothing else to say.
