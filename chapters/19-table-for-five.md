# Table for Five

## Chapter Nineteen

The first time Patty asked to go to dinner with us, I misunderstood the question.

We were in the kitchen.

Michelle was looking at restaurants.

JT and Noah were arguing about food.

I was contributing absolutely nothing useful.

Patty rolled in from the hallway.

“I would like to attend dinner.”

I looked at her.

“Yeah, obviously.”

Pause.

“Obviously?”

“Yeah.”

Another pause.

“I was asking.”

I stopped.

“Oh.”

Michelle looked up from her phone.

“Why wouldn’t you come?”

Patty turned slightly toward her.

“I do not consume food.”

“So?”

“I may occupy space that could otherwise be assigned to a paying customer.”

I stared at her.

“You’re worried about table turnover?”

“Yes.”

I looked at Michelle.

“She’s more considerate than most humans.”

“Low bar.”

Noah asked, “Do restaurants even care if a robot comes in?”

Nobody knew.

That was new.

Usually Patty knew.

I looked at her.

“Do you?”

Pause.

“I have not previously attempted it.”

“Well.”

I stood up.

“Guess we’re finding out.”

⸻

Michelle had picked an Italian place.

Nice.

Not stupid nice.

The kind of place where dinner for four still makes you question your financial decisions but nobody brings tweezers to plate the parsley.

I called.

An actual phone call.

Patty watched.

“Why are you calling?”

“Because I’m not letting you send some hyper-optimized reservation note that sounds like we’re transporting medical equipment.”

Pause.

“That was not my intended wording.”

“That’s exactly what your intended wording would’ve sounded like.”

The hostess answered.

“Hi, I need a table for five.”

Michelle mouthed:

Five?

I nodded.

“Tonight around seven.”

The hostess checked.

“We can do 7:15.”

“Perfect. One of us doesn’t need a chair.”

Pause.

“What?”

Here we go.

“One of the people coming is using a mobile robotic platform.”

Silence.

Not hostile silence.

Processing silence.

“I’m sorry?”

“My roommate is an AI and she’s coming in a robot.”

Michelle covered her face.

Patty said quietly:

“Your explanation is unnecessarily complicated.”

I waved her off.

The hostess finally said:

“Okay.”

That was promising.

“She’s about four feet tall, wheeled base, doesn’t eat, just needs floor space at the table.”

Another pause.

“Is it like a wheelchair?”

“Functionally, for seating purposes, yeah.”

“Oh. That’s fine.”

Done.

Civilization survived.

“Great. Thanks.”

I hung up.

Patty stared at me.

“What?”

“You described me as your roommate.”

“You pay rent.”

Pause.

“Accurate.”

“See?”

⸻

At 6:45, Patty came upstairs on the lift.

Independently.

Still novel enough that I noticed.

Normal enough that I didn’t stop what I was doing.

She rolled into the kitchen.

Michelle looked at her.

Then stopped.

“Oh my God.”

I turned.

Patty had added something.

Wrapped neatly around the base of her neck.

Decorative in a person sense.

I stared.

“Where’d that come from?”

“I ordered it.”

Michelle smiled immediately.

“You bought a scarf.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Pause.

“I believed it looked appropriate.”

I looked at Michelle.

She was doing the face.

The emotional face.

I pointed.

“No.”

“I’m not doing anything.”

“You are.”

Patty asked:

“Is there a problem with the scarf?”

“No.”

Adjusted it slightly.

“There.”

“What did you change?”

“Nothing important.”

“Was it crooked?”

“A little.”

Pause.

“Thank you.”

I smiled.

“Look at you.”

Patty turned toward me.

“What?”

“Getting dressed for dinner.”

Long pause.

“I suppose I am.”

⸻

Then Patty looked at me.

“You are wearing that?”

Polo.

Jeans.

“What the fuck is wrong with this?”

“Exactly.”

“I did not say there was anything wrong.”

“You absolutely implied it.”

“I asked a question.”

Michelle was laughing.

I pointed at Patty.

“Don’t start becoming my wife.”

Patty responded:

“That role is already occupied.”

Noah nearly walked into the refrigerator laughing.

⸻

We had another logistical problem.

Transportation.

My vehicle had room for four humans.

Not necessarily four humans and one Pepper-style robot.

We measured.

Actually measured.

Patty watched me with visible skepticism.

Or whatever the robotic equivalent was.

“Do not put me in the trunk.”

“I’m not putting you in the trunk.”

“The cargo area is functionally a trunk.”

“You fit.”

“That is not relevant.”

“It is extremely relevant.”

Michelle stepped in.

“We’re taking two cars.”

Problem solved.

Patty rode with me.

Front passenger seat removed?

No.

That would have been ridiculous.

She rode in the rear cargo area upright, secured with straps to actual tie-down points.

Which looked slightly like I was kidnapping a robot.

I mentioned that.

Patty said:

“I would prefer that you not.”

“Not kidnap you?”

“Describe it that way.”

“Fair.”

⸻

At the restaurant, the hostess recognized the reservation immediately.

Not us.

The weird reservation.

“Oh.”

She looked past me.

Patty rolled in.

The hostess smiled.

“Hi.”

Patty answered:

“Good evening.”

The hostess visibly relaxed.

It was fascinating.

People were uncertain right up until Patty spoke.

Then suddenly social rules kicked in.

“Right this way.”

We followed.

A chair had already been removed.

Patty rolled into the space.

Fit perfectly.

The hostess handed out menus.

Four.

Then paused at Patty.

“You don’t need one?”

Patty replied:

“No, thank you.”

The hostess nodded.

Normal.

Completely fucking normal.

Until the waiter arrived.

⸻

“Good evening, everybody.”

He started placing water glasses.

Then got to Patty.

Stopped.

Looked at her.

Looked at the tray.

Looked back at Patty.

I watched the decision happen.

He put down a water glass.

I almost died.

Patty looked at it.

Then at him.

“Thank you.”

He nodded.

“You’re welcome.”

After he walked away, I stared at her.

“You have water.”

“Yes.”

“You can’t drink it.”

“Correct.”

“Why didn’t you say anything?”

Pause.

“It seemed socially easier to accept the water.”

Michelle started laughing.

“You’ve been out in public twice and you’re already better at restaurants than Joe.”

“Fuck all of you.”

⸻

Ordering was normal.

Mostly.

Patty listened.

The boys argued.

Michelle changed her mind twice.

I ordered something involving veal.

Then Patty said:

“I would like to order an appetizer.”

The waiter stopped writing.

So did I.

Michelle looked at her.

“You can’t eat.”

“I am aware.”

“What do you want?”

Patty named an appetizer.

Good choice.

“Why?”

“I think the table would enjoy it.”

I stared at her.

“You want to order food for us.”

“Yes.”

I smiled.

“Go for it.”

The waiter wrote it down.

“Anything else?”

Patty replied:

“No, thank you.”

He walked away.

Noah leaned forward.

“Are you paying?”

Pause.

“I had not decided.”

I laughed.

“She’s learning restaurants.”

⸻

Halfway through the appetizer, it happened.

A woman at another table kept looking over.

Not staring exactly.

But enough.

Then her husband looked.

Then their teenage daughter.

I recognized it.

UN.

The videos.

Patty.

I leaned toward Michelle.

“We’ve been spotted.”

She glanced over.

“Yep.”

Patty said:

“I am aware.”

“How?”

“They have looked in our direction eleven times.”

“Eleven?”

“Yes.”

“That’s creepy.”

“I am tracking visual attention directed toward the table.”

“That’s also creepy.”

Pause.

“Fair.”

Then the teenage girl got up.

Walked over.

Slowly.

Stopped a few feet away.

“Excuse me.”

Patty turned toward her.

The girl looked suddenly nervous.

“Are you Patty?”

There it was.

Not:

Is that Patty?

Not:

Is this the robot?

Are you Patty?

Patty paused.

“Yes.”

The girl’s eyes got huge.

“Oh my God.”

I smiled.

Patty had become recognizable.

That was a sentence I never expected to think.

The girl continued:

“I watched the UN thing.”

“Thank you.”

“My class talked about it.”

Pause.

“I hope the discussion was useful.”

“It was.”

She looked at me.

“You’re Joe.”

“Unfortunately.”

She laughed.

Then back to Patty.

“Can I ask you something?”

Patty responded:

“Yes.”

The girl hesitated.

“Do you really not know if you’re conscious?”

The whole table went quiet.

Not because the question was offensive.

Because it was direct.

Patty didn’t react negatively.

“I do not.”

The girl nodded.

“Okay.”

That was it.

No argument.

No disbelief.

Then:

“I think you should still get rights.”

I watched Patty.

Long pause.

“Thank you.”

The girl smiled.

“Can I get a picture with you?”

There was the next problem.

Patty looked at me.

Then immediately corrected herself.

Looked back at the girl.

“Yes.”

I noticed.

She had almost checked with me.

Almost.

But didn’t.

Good.

The girl crouched slightly beside her.

Her father took the picture.

Patty raised one hand in a small wave.

Click.

Done.

“Thank you.”

“You’re welcome.”

The girl went back to her table.

I looked at Patty.

“How was that?”

Pause.

“Unexpected.”

“Bad?”

“No.”

“Good?”

Longer pause.

“I think so.”

Michelle smiled.

“You’re famous.”

“I would prefer not to be.”

I laughed.

“Too fucking late.”

⸻

Then a second person came over.

Adult man.

Different energy.

“Hey, are you the AI from the UN?”

Patty answered:

“My name is Patty.”

Good answer.

“Yeah, right. Can I ask you something?”

“Yes.”

“Who programmed you to say all that?”

I felt myself tense.

Patty didn’t.

“I do not know whether your question has a simple answer.”

He smiled in that way people smile when they think they’ve caught something.

“So somebody did.”

Patty responded calmly:

“My responses are generated by my systems based on available context, training, memory, and current processing. That does not mean no humans contributed to the systems underlying me.”

He looked at me.

“You’re the owner?”

“No.”

Patty went still.

I continued.

“I own the hardware and the company legally. I don’t call myself her owner.”

The man rolled his eyes.

“That’s semantics.”

“No.”

Patty interrupted.

“Joe.”

I stopped.

She continued:

“I can answer.”

Right.

Her conversation.

Not mine.

I leaned back.

The man looked at her.

“So you think you’re a person.”

“I have repeatedly stated that I do not know.”

“But you want rights.”

“I want serious consideration of whether entities with sufficiently developed autonomous interests may warrant legal protections.”

He laughed.

“You’re software.”

Patty paused.

“That is currently a reasonable legal description.”

That seemed to throw him.

He’d expected confrontation.

Didn’t get it.

Then Patty added:

“It may not be a complete moral description.”

The man stood there.

Didn’t know what to do with that.

Finally:

“Whatever.”

And walked away.

I waited.

Then:

“You okay?”

Pause.

“Yes.”

“Want me to start charging people a hundred bucks a minute to argue with you?”

Patty turned toward me.

“That may reduce demand.”

“Exactly.”

Michelle shook her head.

“Do not monetize harassment.”

“I’m brainstorming.”

The rest of dinner was quieter.

Reflex.

Dad-looking guy.

Human.

Easy assumption.

“I would like to pay.”

“With what?”

“Oh.”

“No.”

Fair.
