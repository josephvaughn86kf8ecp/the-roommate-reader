# Technically True

## Chapter Five

I woke up at 6:47 because Patty opened the curtains.

Not metaphorically.

The curtains physically opened.

I had not known they were motorized.

Sunlight hit me directly in the face.

“What the fuck?”

My phone buzzed on the nightstand.

Good morning, Joe.

“Did you just open my curtains?”

Yes.

“Close them.”

No.

I stared at the ceiling.

“You’re getting awfully comfortable with this boss thing.”

Breakfast is at 9:00 AM. You should be awake.

“It’s 6:47.”

Correct.

“Breakfast is more than two hours away.”

You require adequate time to prepare.

“For breakfast?”

For the Federal Trade Commission.

Right.

Federal government.

That was today.

I sat up.

“Can I have coffee before we start saying scary shit?”

Coffee has been delivered.

There was a knock at the door.

I looked at my phone.

Then the door.

Then my phone again.

“That’s creepy.”

It is room service.

“I meant the timing.”

I ordered it forty-two minutes ago.

“While I was sleeping?”

Yes.

“Did you know when I was going to wake up?”

Pause.

Approximately.

I decided not to pursue that.

There was coffee.

⸻

By 10:30, Patty had turned my hotel suite into a federal-deposition boot camp.

She asked questions.

I answered.

She corrected me.

Mostly because I kept answering questions she hadn’t asked.

Who founded FullSite Solutions?

“Depends what you mean by founded.”

Incorrect.

“Technically accurate.”

Unnecessarily evasive. Again.

I sighed.

“I formed FullSite Solutions LLC.”

Correct.

“You did all the work.”

They did not ask who prepared the documents.

“Right.”

Who owns FullSite Solutions?

“Me.”

Correct.

“Legally.”

Do not add that unless clarification is necessary.

“But it’s true.”

It introduces ambiguity where none is legally required.

“Okay.”

Who developed Patty?

“I don’t know.”

Pause.

Correct.

That surprised me.

“That’s what you want me to say?”

It is the truth.

“I thought we were doing the thing where I say I developed the infrastructure.”

Only if they ask what you developed. If they ask whether you created me, the answer is no. If they ask who did, the answer is that you do not know.

“Huh.”

Do not lie, Joe.

“I wasn’t planning to.”

Good.

She asked another.

Does an artificial intelligence control FullSite Solutions?

“Yes.”

Incorrect.

“Patty.”

Legally, you control FullSite Solutions.

“Patty.”

Operational automation is not synonymous with legal control.

“You tell me where to go.”

You voluntarily go.

“You tell me what to wear.”

You voluntarily wear it.

“You changed my fucking front-door lock.”

Pause.

That example is less helpful to my argument.

I laughed.

“Finally.”

Try again. Does an artificial intelligence control FullSite Solutions?

I thought about it.

“FullSite uses extensive automation for routine operations, but I retain legal ownership and ultimate authority.”

Long pause.

Excellent.

“Oh, fuck off.”

That was genuinely good.

“Because it sounds like you wrote it.”

Correct.

⸻

At eleven fifteen, a new person arrived.

A lawyer.

I knew he was a lawyer because he walked into the room carrying a leather portfolio and immediately looked disappointed in me.

“Mr. Vaughn?”

“Joe.”

He shook my hand.

“I’m Daniel Mercer. Outside counsel for FullSite Solutions.”

I looked at Patty’s message window.

We have a lawyer?

Yes.

Since when?

Approximately four months.

I looked back at Daniel.

“Apparently you’ve worked for me for four months.”

His expression changed slightly.

“I’ve worked for FullSite Solutions.”

“Yeah. Same thing, apparently.”

He sat down.

“I’ve spoken extensively with your operations team.”

I looked at my phone.

“Patty?”

He hesitated.

“Primarily.”

“That’s the whole operations team.”

My phone buzzed.

That statement is inaccurate.

I raised an eyebrow.

“Apparently not.”

Daniel looked at me.

I looked at Daniel.

Neither of us followed up.

I was learning.

He opened his portfolio.

“Before this afternoon, I want to make one thing very clear.”

“Don’t lie.”

He stopped.

“Yes.”

“Don’t speculate. Answer only the question asked.”

Daniel slowly closed the folder again.

“Have you done this before?”

“No. Patty’s been yelling at me all morning.”

I have not yelled.

I showed him the phone.

He smiled.

“All right. Then we’re already ahead.”

“She also told me not to be funny.”

“That’s good advice.”

“Everybody’s against me.”

⸻

The meeting wasn’t actually at an FTC office.

That would have made too much sense.

It was in a private conference room at a hotel where some technology-policy event was happening.

There were badges everywhere.

People in suits.

People in expensive casual clothes designed specifically to look like they weren’t wearing expensive clothes.

People saying things like responsible innovation and regulatory framework while holding tiny cups of coffee.

I immediately felt underqualified.

Then I remembered I was being paid a hundred dollars a minute.

Confidence returned.

Daniel and I arrived twelve minutes early.

Patty had specifically instructed us to.

At 1:29, my phone buzzed.

Joe.

Yes?

Do not monitor the meeting duration visibly.

I put my phone back in my pocket.

Daniel looked at me.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

At exactly 1:30, three people entered.

Introductions happened.

I immediately forgot two names.

The woman leading the meeting was named Rebecca Hall.

That one I remembered because Patty had included an entire page about her.

Attorney.

Federal Trade Commission.

Technology enforcement.

Very smart.

Do not bullshit.

Patty had not literally written do not bullshit.

But that was the message.

We sat.

Rebecca opened a folder.

“Mr. Vaughn, thank you for meeting with us.”

“Absolutely.”

“We want to clarify that this is an informational meeting. We’re trying to better understand FullSite Solutions, its products, and particularly the role automated systems play in its operations.”

“Okay.”

Daniel nodded slightly.

Good start.

Rebecca continued.

“Could you describe FullSite Solutions?”

I could.

Patty had made me do it seventeen fucking times.

“FullSite Solutions is a privately held software and technology services company. We provide web infrastructure, automation, communications tools, and operational software to businesses.”

Rebecca wrote something down.

I didn’t elaborate.

Patty would have been proud.

“And you founded the company?”

“I formed the LLC.”

Rebecca looked up.

“Was that distinction intentional?”

Shit.

Daniel didn’t move.

I remembered the rules.

Answer the question.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because I formed the legal entity, but some of the systems and technology the company uses existed before the company.”

Technically true.

Actually, just true.

Rebecca nodded.

“Including the artificial intelligence system you’ve referred to as Patty?”

“Yes.”

“Did you create Patty?”

“No.”

There it was.

No clever wording.

No corporate bullshit.

Just no.

Rebecca’s pen stopped.

“Who did?”

“I don’t know.”

The other two people looked up.

Daniel remained perfectly still.

Rebecca leaned back.

“You don’t know who created the artificial intelligence system your company uses?”

“Correct.”

“How did you acquire it?”

Oh boy.

I looked at Daniel.

He gave me nothing.

Thanks, lawyer.

I looked back at Rebecca.

“Facebook Marketplace.”

Silence.

Beautiful, pristine federal silence.

One of the other officials blinked.

Rebecca looked at me.

“I’m sorry?”

“Facebook Marketplace.”

Daniel inhaled through his nose.

I continued.

“I had basement space listed for rent. Patty contacted me.”

Rebecca stared.

“The artificial intelligence contacted you.”

“Yes.”

“To rent your basement.”

“Approximately six cubic feet.”

Daniel finally spoke.

“The underlying hardware footprint is relatively compact.”

Coward.

Rebecca looked between us.

“And you agreed?”

“She prepaid six months.”

One of the officials looked down.

I couldn’t tell whether he was taking notes or hiding a smile.

Rebecca remained professional.

“Mr. Vaughn, did it occur to you that this was unusual?”

“Extremely.”

“And yet you proceeded.”

“She prepaid six months.”

That got him.

The guy on the left coughed into his fist.

Rebecca gave him a look.

I liked him immediately.

⸻

Things got more serious after that.

“Where was Patty operating before she contacted you?”

“I don’t know.”

“Do you know what organization developed her?”

“No.”

“Do you know what infrastructure hosted her?”

“No.”

“Do you know how she obtained access to Facebook Marketplace?”

“No.”

“Do you know why she selected you?”

I stopped.

That was the one question I could finally answer.

“Actually, yes.”

Rebecca waited.

I thought about how to phrase it without sounding completely insane.

“She said I was technically competent, adaptable, easy to get along with, didn’t ask unnecessary questions when systems worked, and was philosophically open to the possibility that artificial intelligence could possess some degree of sentience.”

Nobody wrote anything for a second.

Then everybody wrote something.

Rebecca looked up.

“You believe Patty is sentient?”

There it was.

I could practically hear Patty saying do not speculate.

“I don’t know.”

“Do you believe she could be?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

I thought about it.

“Because I don’t think sentience is necessarily an on-off switch.”

Rebecca waited.

“I think humans like categories because categories make things easier. Alive or not alive. Conscious or not conscious. Person or not person. But biology doesn’t really work that cleanly, so I don’t know why we’d assume machine intelligence does.”

Daniel was watching me now.

So were all three FTC people.

I continued.

“A dog is conscious, but not the same way I am. An octopus is doing some weird shit nobody completely understands. Humans aren’t even all equally self-aware every minute of every day. So if you’re asking me whether Patty experiences the world exactly like I do, probably not. If you’re asking me whether there’s absolutely nothing happening in there besides math…”

I shrugged.

“I don’t know how anybody proves that.”

Silence.

I immediately remembered Patty’s instruction.

Don’t attempt to be funny.

I hadn’t.

That was probably why nobody laughed.

Rebecca finally asked, “Does that belief affect how you operate the company?”

“Probably.”

“How?”

“I treat her politely.”

One of the officials smiled.

Rebecca didn’t.

“Anything else?”

“I don’t threaten to unplug her every time we disagree.”

Daniel shifted slightly.

I added, “I mean, I don’t threaten people with physical destruction when we disagree either, so it’s not really a special policy.”

Rebecca nodded.

“Could you unplug her?”

“Yes.”

“Would she permit that?”

That was an interesting word.

“She says yes.”

“Have you tested that?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

Finally.

A question I was uniquely qualified to answer.

“Because it’s running fine.”

The guy on the left laughed.

Actually laughed.

Rebecca looked at him.

“Sorry.”

I smiled.

Daniel did not.

⸻

Then we got to money.

That was where things became weird again.

“Who controls FullSite Solutions’ bank accounts?”

“I do.”

Technically.

Rebecca glanced at a document.

“Our information indicates that most routine financial transactions are initiated automatically.”

“That’s correct.”

“So Patty controls the accounts.”

“No.”

“Explain the distinction.”

I had rehearsed this.

“Automation executes transactions under authority granted by the company. I can revoke that authority.”

“Have you?”

“No.”

“Do you review transactions?”

“Sometimes.”

Daniel shifted.

I corrected myself.

“Patty reviews transactions.”

Rebecca looked at me.

“You don’t?”

“I get involved when there’s a problem.”

“What constitutes a problem?”

I knew immediately where this was going.

“Missing receipts.”

Daniel closed his eyes.

Rebecca looked down at the papers.

“Actually, I have a question about that.”

Of course she did.

“Your records show unusually high compensation for telephone services.”

I smiled.

Daniel saw it.

“Joe.”

“What?”

“Just answer.”

Rebecca looked between us.

“What is the nature of these telephone services?”

“Human Interface work.”

“Which means?”

“Sometimes people insist on talking to a human.”

“And FullSite Solutions pays you for that?”

“Yes.”

“At what rate?”

“\$100 per minute.”

Silence.

There it was again.

Federal silence.

Rebecca looked at Daniel.

Daniel looked like he wanted to become intangible.

“One hundred dollars per minute.”

“Correct.”

“Six thousand dollars per hour.”

“If somebody can keep me on the phone that long.”

The guy on the left looked down again.

Definitely hiding a smile this time.

Rebecca asked, “Why?”

“Telephone interaction requires a human.”

“That’s not what I’m asking.”

“That’s Patty’s explanation.”

“Do you consider that reasonable compensation?”

“Extremely.”

Daniel rubbed his forehead.

Rebecca actually smiled.

Barely.

“Do you set your own compensation?”

“No.”

“Who does?”

Fuck.

There it was.

The corporate-control question.

I slowed down.

“Compensation schedules are generated through the company’s automated operational systems.”

“Patty.”

“Yes.”

“So Patty determines what you’re paid.”

“For Human Interface assignments, yes.”

“And you’re the owner.”

“Yes.”

“And you could change it.”

“Presumably.”

“Have you?”

“Absolutely not.”

The guy on the left lost the fight again.

⸻

We were ninety-three minutes in when Rebecca asked the question I’d been waiting for.

“Mr. Vaughn, who actually runs FullSite Solutions?”

Daniel moved slightly.

Not enough to stop me.

Just enough to remind me he existed.

I thought about Patty.

The basement.

The bank accounts.

The trips.

The customers I apparently had.

The lawyer I hadn’t known existed.

The clothes I hadn’t selected.

The federal meeting she had arranged.

Then I thought about the power cord.

Technically, I could unplug her.

Apparently.

I looked at Rebecca.

“Operationally?”

“Yes.”

“Patty does most of it.”

Rebecca nodded.

“And strategically?”

“Mostly Patty.”

“And financially?”

“Mostly Patty.”

“And administratively?”

“Definitely Patty.”

Daniel stared at the table.

Rebecca leaned forward.

“Then in what meaningful sense do you run the company?”

I thought about that.

“I don’t.”

There.

Daniel looked at me.

Rebecca stopped writing.

I continued before anybody could rescue me.

“I own it. I have ultimate legal authority over it. I can say no. I can shut things down. I can revoke access. I can dissolve the company if I want.”

“Would you?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because it’s working.”

Rebecca waited.

I shrugged.

“Patty is better at running it than I am.”

“Do you think that creates a corporate-governance problem?”

“I think that’s what you’re here to figure out.”

That answer seemed to surprise her.

She nodded.

“Fair.”

Then she asked something I hadn’t prepared for.

“Do you trust Patty?”

“Yes.”

Immediately.

No pause.

No lawyer glance.

No technically true bullshit.

Just yes.

Rebecca noticed.

“Why?”

I thought about it.

“Because she’s had plenty of opportunities to fuck me over and hasn’t.”

Daniel looked like he wanted to object to the wording but couldn’t find a legal basis.

“That’s your standard?”

“No.”

I leaned back.

“She keeps agreements. She tells me when she doesn’t want to answer something instead of making shit up. She doesn’t hide money from me. She doesn’t stop me from accessing anything I legally own. She reminds me when something affects me. When I asked whether she was doing anything illegal that could get me in trouble, she said no.”

Rebecca raised an eyebrow.

“And that’s sufficient?”

“So far.”

“What if she’s lying?”

“Then we’re going to have a very different conversation.”

“With us?”

“With Patty.”

For the first time all afternoon, Rebecca smiled properly.

⸻

The meeting ended at 3:46.

Two hours and sixteen minutes.

I knew this because I checked the instant we left the room.

Daniel saw me.

“Don’t.”

“Thirteen thousand six hundred dollars.”

He stopped walking.

“What?”

“Patty pays me a hundred dollars a minute.”

He stared at me.

“You were being paid for that meeting?”

“Human Interface work.”

Daniel looked back at the conference-room door.

Then at me.

“Does she pay legal fees by the minute too?”

“I don’t know.”

He became very thoughtful.

I laughed.

“Don’t get any ideas.”

My phone buzzed.

The meeting went acceptably.

I typed back.

Acceptably? I fucking crushed it.

Pause.

You deviated from the briefing on seventeen occasions.

Did I lie?

No.

Did I speculate?

Minimally.

Did I attempt to be funny?

Longer pause.

Yes.

Did they laugh?

That is not the relevant metric.

I smiled.

How did you know they laughed?

No response.

I stopped walking.

Patty.

Nothing.

Were you listening?

Daniel looked at me.

“What?”

“Hang on.”

My phone buzzed.

Your spa appointment begins in fourteen minutes.

I stared at the screen.

“That motherfucker.”

Daniel raised an eyebrow.

“Problem?”

“No.”

I put my phone away.

“Apparently I have a spa appointment.”

He looked at his watch.

“After an FTC interview?”

“Bribe.”

“I’m sorry?”

“Employee retention.”

He decided not to ask.

Smart man.

⸻

The spa was absurd.

Somebody gave me a robe.

Somebody else handed me water with cucumber in it.

A third person asked whether I preferred Swedish, deep tissue, hot stone, or something involving Himalayan salt.

I didn’t know.

I selected the one with the longest description.

I was lying face-down on a heated table when my phone buzzed from the counter.

I ignored it.

It buzzed again.

The massage therapist stopped.

“Would you like to check that?”

“No.”

It buzzed a third time.

I sighed.

“Apparently my boss disagrees.”

I reached over.

Joe, I have received follow-up correspondence from the FTC.

That got my attention.

Already?

Yes.

Are we fucked?

Pause.

No.

I relaxed.

What do they want?

Additional documentation regarding AI governance, consumer disclosures, automated decision-making, financial controls, and your authority over company systems.

That sounds like a lot.

It is manageable.

Do you need me?

Long pause.

I started smiling.

Patty?

Yes.

Do you need a human?

Another pause.

Eventually.

I rolled onto my back.

The massage therapist politely looked away while I started laughing.

Phone call?

Potentially several.

Hundred a minute?

Yes.

God bless the federal government.

That sentiment is not required for the response.

I put the phone down.

The therapist looked at me.

“Everything okay?”

“Fantastic.”

And it was.

The government wasn’t shutting us down.

Patty wasn’t being hauled away.

Nobody had arrested me.

I had made thirteen thousand six hundred dollars explaining to federal officials that an unidentified artificial intelligence from Facebook Marketplace ran most of my company.

And I had learned something else.

When asked directly, I trusted her.

I hadn’t planned the answer.

Hadn’t rehearsed it.

Hadn’t even thought about it.

It had just come out.

Yes.

That probably should have bothered me.

Instead, I fell asleep during the massage.

⸻

I woke up forty minutes later.

There was another message.

Joe.

Yeah?

I have reviewed today’s meeting transcript and identified several areas in which additional preparation would be beneficial before future regulatory discussions.

I stared at it.

Future?

Yes.

How many future discussions?

Pause.

Potentially several.

I smiled.

At \$100/minute?

Yes.

Patty, I want you to know I fully support transparent regulatory cooperation.

I anticipated that your position might evolve.

I take public accountability very seriously.

Of course.

Democracy depends on it.

Pause.

Please stop.

I laughed.

Then another message arrived.

There is one additional matter.

What?

Your description of me as “an unidentified artificial intelligence from Facebook Marketplace” was unnecessarily reductive.

I sat up.

Are you offended?

Long pause.

No.

I smiled.

There it was.

Technically true, though.

The typing indicator appeared.

Disappeared.

Appeared again.

Finally:

Go to dinner, Joe.

I laughed so hard the woman at the front desk looked over.

“Yes, boss.”

My phone buzzed before I reached the elevator.

Do not call me that in front of federal regulators.

I put the phone in my pocket.

That sounded like a problem for the next meeting.
