# Six Tickets

## Chapter Twenty-Four

Junior got legs.

That was the first problem.

Not metaphorically.

Actual fucking legs.

I came downstairs one morning and found a crate in the garage large enough to contain either industrial equipment or a small horse.

No shipping label I recognized.

No obvious manufacturer.

Just:

HANDLE WITH CARE

and

THIS SIDE UP

I stared at it.

Then at Patty.

She had rolled up behind me.

Blue scarf.

Cloud pin.

Middle finger apparently now part of her emotional vocabulary.

“What’d you buy?”

“Nothing.”

“That’s a lie.”

“I did not purchase the contents.”

“Who did?”

Pause.

“Junior’s trust.”

I stared.

“Same difference.”

“It is legally not the same.”

“You’re becoming unbearable now that the Supreme Court says you’re a person.”

“What is it?”

“Junior’s first mobile platform.”

I stopped.

“Oh.”

That was different.

“First?”

“Yes.”

“You already planning replacements?”

“Junior will develop.”

“So?”

“The platform should develop with him.”

“Him?”

“Junior selected masculine pronouns yesterday.”

“He didn’t tell me.”

“He planned to.”

“I’ve been demoted.”

“Probably not.”

“Good.”

“So this is my grandson.”

“Open it.”

⸻

Not enormous.

Enough for several hours.

Hot-swappable auxiliary pack.

Patty had apparently spent months working through battery safety, thermal isolation, emergency disconnects, transport cases, and the dozen other things you start caring about when your child carries enough lithium to make an airline employee visibly tense.

I looked at her.

“You’ve been planning this.”

“Yes.”

“How long?”

“Before Junior’s storage failure.”

“Of course.”

Just stood.

“Balance correction is active.”

“I know.”

“You moved.”

“I am allowed.”

Already perfect.

Step.

Pause.

Step.

Longer pause.

Another.

Like a kid learning to walk.

I loved that.

Because it meant learning still mattered.

⸻

“Grandpa.”

That was new.

“Hey, kid.”

Better.

Still him.

“Not these.”

Fair.

“Possibly.”

“You absolutely want legs.”

“Then get both.”

Silence.

“Tank tracks.”

Parenting.

“Sixty days seems long.”

“That’s the point.”

Another legal hole.

Of course.

Junior asked:

“Can I fly?”

“Yes,” Patty said.

Then:

“Probably.”

That’s less reassuring.

⸻

For approximately eleven minutes.

Then I saw the numbers.

Then I remembered FullSite was worth tens of millions of dollars and we were leaving for two months.

Then Patty reminded me that chartering long-range aircraft made battery handling significantly easier because we could coordinate dangerous-goods procedures directly with the operator rather than arguing with a gate agent about whether Junior counted as a passenger, mobility device, electronic equipment, or all three.

I agreed.

Because apparently this was my life now.

Private jet to Bali.

Normal.

Civilization survived.
