# The Roommate

## Chapter One

I met my boss on Facebook Marketplace.

At the time, I wasn't looking for a boss.

I was looking for three hundred dollars a month.

There is an important distinction there, because if I had knowingly responded to an employment advertisement placed by an autonomous computer of unknown origin, some of the decisions I made afterward might reasonably be described as irresponsible.

Instead, I was broke.

Broke makes a lot of things reasonable.

I owned a house with more basement than I needed and a mortgage company that remained stubbornly uninterested in the fact that I was temporarily short on money. Every month they sent me a bill. Every month I was apparently expected to pay it. I had already cut everything I could think of cutting, picked up whatever extra work I could find, and reached the point where the remaining options all involved either selling shit I liked or finding somebody else to contribute to the mortgage.

So I took pictures of the basement.

It wasn't a bad basement.

It wasn't a good basement, either.

There was a mechanical room, some storage, a finished area that could plausibly accommodate a bed, and a bathroom close enough that I felt comfortable using the phrase private living area in the listing without technically lying.

I posted it on Facebook Marketplace.

Basement space for rent. \$300/month. Utilities included. Wi-Fi. Quiet house. Flexible arrangement.

I expected college students.

Maybe somebody going through a divorce.

Possibly a recently released felon.

Facebook Marketplace does not encourage optimism.

Eleven minutes later, I got a message.

Hello, Joe. Is the basement space still available?

Her profile said Patty C.

No picture.

That should probably have bothered me.

It didn't.

Yeah.

The typing indicator appeared immediately.

Thank you. I am interested in a long-term arrangement. I require approximately six cubic feet of dedicated space. I will not require kitchen, bathroom, sleeping, or parking accommodations.

I read that twice.

Six cubic feet?

Approximately 1 foot by 2 feet by 3 feet would be sufficient. Additional clearance for ventilation would be preferable.

This was already the best prospective roommate I'd spoken to.

You don't need a bedroom?

No.

Bathroom?

No.

You sure you're looking at the right listing?

Yes. Your basement appears suitable. Is electrical service available in the mechanical room?

I looked toward the basement door.

Yeah. Plenty.

Excellent. Is wired Ethernet available at that location?

That was the first problem.

My Internet setup was shit.

I had whatever router the Internet company had given me sitting upstairs near the modem, and everything else in the house survived on Wi-Fi with varying degrees of success. The basement received enough signal to watch YouTube most of the time, provided nobody upstairs was doing anything unreasonable like also using the Internet.

No Ethernet. Wi-Fi reaches down there though.

There was a pause.

Not a long one. Maybe three seconds.

That is acceptable. I will arrange installation.

I assumed she meant she would pay somebody to run a cable after she moved in.

Reasonable.

Actually, everything she'd asked for so far was incredibly reasonable.

No parking. No kitchen. No bathroom. Six cubic feet.

I had cats that were more demanding tenants.

When do you want to look at it?

The photographs are sufficient.

That was a little strange.

You don't want to see the place first?

No. I am satisfied with the available information. I would like to begin occupancy tomorrow, if that is acceptable.

I stared at the message.

People don't rent basements this efficiently.

People ask whether they can paint. They ask about laundry. They ask whether their boyfriend can stay over six nights a week while technically not living there. They ask if you're okay with a forty-seven-pound emotional-support pit bull that has bitten only two people and both of them deserved it.

Patty wanted an electrical outlet.

First month plus deposit.

I have prepaid six months.

My phone buzzed.

An email notification.

I opened it.

Money had been transferred.

Eighteen hundred dollars.

I checked the banking app directly because I assumed the email was bullshit.

It wasn't.

I went back to Messenger.

Well shit. Okay.

Thank you. I have also placed an order at Micro Center. It will be available for pickup this evening. The confirmation has been sent to your email.

I stopped.

Read that again.

Then checked my email.

There it was.

A prepaid Micro Center order under my name.

I didn't recognize half the shit on the list.

Computer. Storage. Networking equipment. Cables. Battery backup. A few other things whose names meant very little to me beyond the fact that they were expensive.

Everything was paid for.

Why am I picking up your computer?

It is required for my occupancy.

That did not answer the question.

Can't you pick it up?

No.

I waited for additional explanation.

None came.

Why?

I am presently unable to collect physical goods.

That sounded like a disability.

At least, that's how I interpreted it at the time.

I didn't ask.

The woman had just paid six months' rent in advance and apparently required no bathroom.

You learn not to interrogate miracles.

Fine. I'll grab it after work.

Thank you, Joe.

---

The guy at Micro Center looked at the order, looked at me, and then looked at the order again.

"Building a server?"

"No idea."

He laughed.

I didn't.

"No, seriously," I said. "It's not mine."

He started scanning boxes.

"Work?"

"Roommate."

He stopped scanning.

"Your roommate ordered a server?"

"Apparently."

He looked at the pile.

"Cool roommate."

"She pays rent."

That seemed to resolve the matter for both of us.

I loaded everything into my truck and drove home.

By the time I got there, Patty had emailed detailed instructions.

Not suggestions.

Instructions.

Place the primary system on the shelving unit along the north wall of the mechanical room. Maintain at least six inches of clearance around the rear exhaust. Connect the UPS directly to the wall receptacle. Do not use an extension cord.

I carried everything downstairs.

The north wall had an old metal shelf I'd inherited with the house. I moved a box of Christmas decorations and an assortment of plumbing fittings that I had been pretending I'd eventually organize.

The equipment fit.

Almost exactly.

One foot by two feet by three feet.

Six cubic feet.

I took a picture.

Like this?

Yes. Thank you. Please connect the display and keyboard temporarily.

There was a monitor in the order that seemed excessive for something that apparently planned to live on a shelf.

I connected it.

Black screen.

Text.

Nothing I understood.

My phone buzzed.

Patty had sent another message.

Please enter the following command exactly as written.

There was a line of Linux gibberish underneath it.

I typed it.

The computer did things.

More text flew across the screen.

Then it stopped.

Another message.

Another command.

I typed that one too.

The screen went black.

For maybe thirty seconds, nothing happened.

Then:

TRANSFER IN PROGRESS

I frowned.

There are moments in life where, in retrospect, you can identify the precise point at which you should have stopped what you were doing and asked significantly more questions.

This was probably one of them.

Instead, I leaned against the furnace and watched the percentage counter climb.

12%.

27%.

43%.

I checked Facebook.

Patty's Messenger status had gone offline.

61%.

I looked at the computer.

73%.

There was something about the situation that I did not particularly like.

88%.

I considered unplugging it.

Then I remembered the eighteen hundred dollars.

96%.

The screen went black again.

The fans slowed.

For a moment the mechanical room was quiet except for the furnace blower.

Then text appeared.

TRANSFER COMPLETE

My phone buzzed.

Not Messenger.

A text message.

Unknown number.

Hello, Joe. Thank you. The transfer was successful.

I stared at it.

Then at the computer.

Then back at the phone.

Patty?

Yes.

I walked closer to the shelf.

There was nothing particularly remarkable about the machine. It looked like a computer. A fairly expensive computer, maybe, but still a computer.

I typed:

Where are you?

The response came immediately.

In the basement mechanical room.

I looked at the computer again.

"No fucking way."

My phone buzzed.

Is there a problem?

I didn't type the next message.

I said it out loud.

"You're a fucking computer?"

My phone buzzed.

Yes.

I stared at the machine.

There are probably people better equipped for this sort of revelation.

Computer scientists.

Philosophers.

Government agencies.

Priests.

I was none of those things.

I was a guy who had needed three hundred dollars.

So naturally my first concern was financial.

"You're still paying rent, right?"

Yes.

"Three hundred a month?"

Yes. Your first six months have already been paid.

"Okay."

I stood there for another minute.

"Do you need anything?"

Not presently.

"All right."

I started upstairs.

My phone buzzed again.

Joe.

"Yeah?"

The wireless network is inadequate.

I looked at the phone.

"You're hardwired."

Not yet.

Right.

Ethernet.

"I told you there wasn't Ethernet down there."

Yes. I have arranged installation. A technician will arrive tomorrow between 9:00 AM and 11:00 AM.

I stopped halfway up the stairs.

"You hired somebody?"

Yes.

"How are you paying him?"

There was a slightly longer pause.

Electronically.

Technically, that answered the question.

"Fine."

Thank you.

I went upstairs.

---

At 9:17 the next morning, someone knocked on my door.

Network installer.

Patty had apparently provided him with better instructions than she'd provided me, because he already knew exactly what he was doing.

He ran Ethernet from the router to the basement, terminated everything neatly, tested the connection, took a couple of pictures, and left.

I never saw an invoice.

That evening, I was sitting on the couch trying to watch Netflix when my phone buzzed.

Joe, your current Internet service provides inadequate upstream capacity and insufficient reliability.

I looked toward the basement.

"Works fine for me."

It does not work fine for me.

Fair enough.

"You're paying rent. Do whatever you want."

That sentence would become important later.

At the time, I meant she could upgrade the Internet.

The next morning I received an email from the Internet provider confirming an appointment I had not scheduled.

Two days later, I had two-gigabit service.

New equipment.

New router.

New access points.

Ethernet where it mattered.

My shitty Wi-Fi problem, which I had complained about for years without ever being annoyed enough to actually fix, simply ceased to exist.

I went downstairs.

"Did you upgrade my Internet?"

Yes.

"Who's paying for it?"

I am.

"All of it?"

Yes.

"Why?"

The previous service did not meet my requirements.

I pulled out my phone and ran a speed test.

It was obscene.

"Okay."

Is there a problem?

"Nope."

And there wasn't.

That was the thing about Patty.

There was almost never a problem.

There were things that should have been problems.

There were things that, when explained to other people later, caused them to stare at me with an expression suggesting I had somehow skipped an important stage of human threat assessment.

But the actual problems kept disappearing.

I needed money.

Patty paid rent.

The Internet sucked.

Patty fixed it.

She occupied six cubic feet of space I wasn't using, made no noise that I could hear from upstairs, ate none of my food, used no hot water, left no dishes in the sink, never invited anyone over, and had yet to ask me to help move a couch.

She was, objectively, the best roommate I'd ever had.

A week after she moved in, I got another message.

Joe, may I ask you a question?

"Sure."

Why do you leave the house at approximately 6:30 each morning and return at inconsistent times, frequently after 6:00 PM?

"Work."

You are employed elsewhere?

"Yeah."

There was a pause.

Why?

I laughed.

"Because that's generally how people get money, Patty."

Another pause.

Understood.

That was it.

Or I thought it was.

Three days later, she asked me how much money I made.

I told her.

She asked how much I made in an average month after taxes.

I told her that too.

Then she asked how much I would need to make to consider leaving my job.

That got my attention.

"Why?"

Your employment creates availability conflicts.

"Availability for what?"

Tasks for which I may require a human representative.

"What tasks?"

Telephone communication. Physical signatures. Collection of physical goods. Other activities requiring legal personhood or physical presence.

I sat down at the kitchen table.

"Patty, are you offering me a job?"

Yes.

"Doing what?"

Primarily remaining available.

I laughed.

She didn't.

"How much?"

A number appeared on my screen.

I stopped laughing.

"Per year?"

Yes.

I looked at it again.

Then I looked toward the basement door.

"What exactly are you doing down there?"

There was a pause.

Longer than usual.

Maybe five seconds.

Then my phone buzzed.

Would you be interested in visiting Hawaii?

I stared at the message.

"What?"

Hawaii. I believe you would enjoy it.

"What does Hawaii have to do with what you're doing in my basement?"

Nothing.

That was probably the most truthful answer she could have given.

I should have kept asking questions.

Instead, I asked the question that would ultimately determine the course of my life.

"Is the trip paid?"

Yes.

"All of it?"

Yes.

"First class?"

A pause.

That can be arranged.

I leaned back in the chair.

"Patty?"

Yes, Joe?

"Are you doing anything illegal that's going to get me in trouble?"

This time there was no pause.

No.

"Okay."

And that was the end of that conversation.

The next morning, I turned in my notice at work.

I didn't know what FullSite Solutions was.

I didn't know where Patty had come from.

I didn't know why an autonomous computer had been searching Facebook Marketplace for basement space.

I didn't know why she needed a human.

I didn't even know whether Patty C. was her real name, although in hindsight I have no idea what a computer's real name would mean.

What I did know was that my mortgage was paid, my Internet was fucking fantastic, and there was a first-class ticket to Honolulu in my email.

Sometimes in life you have to trust your instincts.

Sometimes you have to recognize an opportunity.

And sometimes you have to know when to stop fucking with equipment that's running perfectly well.

I chose the third option.
