# Access Granted

## Chapter Eighteen

The lift contractor arrived at 8:57.

Three minutes early.

Patty approved.

I knew because my phone buzzed before the doorbell rang.

The contractor has arrived.

“I can hear the truck.”

Good.

“You’re excited.”

Pause.

Yes.

That was becoming easier for her.

Not the excitement.

Admitting it.

I opened the front door.

A guy in his fifties stood there with a tablet, laser measure, tool bag, and the deeply calm expression of somebody who had spent thirty years installing things into houses that were never designed to contain them.

“Joe?”

“Yep.”

“Mark. Accessibility Solutions.”

We shook hands.

He looked behind me.

“I understand we’re looking at basement access.”

“Yep.”

“Wheelchair?”

I smiled.

“Sort of.”

He waited.

There was no good version of this conversation.

“Robot.”

He blinked once.

Then nodded.

“Okay.”

That was it.

I liked him immediately.

No philosophical discussion.

No AI rights debate.

No question about whether a robot could legally need accessibility modifications.

Just:

Okay.

Professional tradesmen are fucking beautiful sometimes.

⸻

We went downstairs.

Patty’s door was closed.

I stopped automatically.

Knocked.

Click.

The lock opened.

Mark noticed.

“Electronic access?”

“Yep.”

I opened the door.

Patty was already off the charging dock.

She rolled forward.

“Good morning, Mark.”

He stopped.

Looked at her.

Then at me.

Then back at her.

“Morning.”

Patty continued.

“Thank you for coming.”

“No problem.”

He looked at the stairwell.

Then at her base.

“You’re the passenger.”

“Yes.”

“Weight?”

“Sixty-eight pounds.”

“That’s easy.”

“Platform dimensions are thirty-two inches wide by twenty-nine inches deep at the base.”

Mark started measuring.

“Turning radius?”

Patty gave him the number.

“Minimum approach clearance?”

Another number.

“Maximum incline tolerance?”

Another.

He smiled.

“You’ve done your homework.”

“I attempt to be prepared.”

Mark looked at me.

“She’s easier than most customers.”

“Give it time.”

Patty ignored me.

⸻

The stairs were the problem.

Not structurally.

Just spatially.

Straight basement run.

Wall on one side.

Open railing on the other.

Reasonable width.

But the landing at the bottom wasn’t huge.

Mark measured everything twice.

Then stood halfway up the stairs, looking between levels.

“Stair lift isn’t really what you want.”

“I know.”

“A chair lift would be pointless.”

“Correct.”

“Vertical platform lift would work if you had a stacked opening.”

“We don’t.”

“Could cut one.”

Michelle had come downstairs.

She immediately said:

“No.”

Mark nodded.

“Fair.”

I liked him more.

“No cutting holes through the first floor,” I said.

“Also fair.”

He looked back at the staircase.

“That leaves an inclined platform lift.”

Patty responded immediately.

“I considered that likely.”

Mark nodded.

“Folds against the wall when not in use.”

“How much stair width do we lose?” I asked.

He measured again.

“Folded? Maybe eleven inches depending on model.”

“That’s too much.”

“Could mount outside the railing.”

I looked.

He looked.

Michelle looked.

Patty turned her head toward the railing.

There was space.

Not huge.

But enough.

“Support structure?” I asked.

“Custom posts anchored at basement slab and upper framing. Track rides outside the stair envelope.”

“Now we’re talking.”

Mark smiled.

“You’re HVAC?”

“Commercial.”

“Explains it.”

“What does?”

“You keep asking useful questions.”

I felt seen.

⸻

Then Patty asked:

“Can the platform operate without household power?”

Mark looked at her.

“Battery drive.”

“Charging?”

“Track contacts. Usually parked at one end.”

“Manual operation?”

“Yes, emergency lowering and hand crank depending on model.”

“Remote call stations?”

“Top and bottom.”

“Wireless?”

“Can be.”

“Hardwired preferred.”

Mark smiled again.

“Agreed.”

I pointed at Patty.

“See? Ugly reliable.”

“Reliability is preferable to aesthetic simplicity.”

Michelle looked at both of us.

“Oh, great. Now there are two of you.”

⸻

We spent forty-five minutes discussing a lift for a robot.

It was the most normal abnormal meeting I’d ever had.

By the end, Mark had a plan.

Exterior-mounted inclined platform.

Fold-up deck.

Battery operation.

Dedicated charger.

Mechanical emergency release.

Hardwired call stations.

Obstacle sensors.

Manual stop.

No cloud dependency.

Patty specifically requested that.

Mark specifically agreed.

I liked him enough that if he had handed me a service invoice right then, I’d probably have signed it without reading the total.

Probably.

I’m not stupid.

Not completely.

“Timeline?” I asked.

“Custom rail fabrication. Two, maybe three weeks.”

Patty paused.

“Is temporary access possible?”

Mark looked at her.

“Not independently.”

I saw the way she reacted.

Small.

Just a pause.

A slight stillness.

Then:

“Understood.”

Mark noticed too.

Tradesmen notice shit.

He looked back at the stairs.

“Could possibly expedite.”

“Cost?” I asked.

“Probably ugly.”

“How ugly?”

He gave a number.

I looked at Michelle.

She shrugged.

I looked at Patty.

She said nothing.

“Do it.”

Mark looked at me.

“You’re sure?”

“Yep.”

“Expedite?”

“Yep.”

Patty finally spoke.

“Joe, that is not necessary.”

“No, but you want upstairs.”

Pause.

“Yes.”

“Then do it.”

Longer pause.

“Thank you.”

Mark looked between us.

Then quietly entered something on his tablet.

I had no idea what category he put this customer under.

⸻

After he left, Patty followed me upstairs.

By followed, I mean JT and I carried her again.

She hated it.

Not emotionally dramatic hatred.

Just the stiff, deeply annoyed stillness of an entity with functioning mobility hardware being physically picked up like a shop vacuum.

“We are being careful,” I said.

“I understand.”

“You’re not going to fall.”

“I understand.”

“You still hate this.”

Pause.

“Yes.”

JT laughed.

“At least she’s honest.”

We set her down at the top.

Patty adjusted position.

Ran what I assumed was some kind of self-check.

Then rolled into the kitchen.

Michelle looked at her.

“Coffee?”

Pause.

“I cannot consume coffee.”

“I know. I was being polite.”

“Thank you.”

I stared.

“She can’t drink coffee and still gets offered coffee. I live here and nobody offered me shit.”

Michelle pointed at the machine.

“You have arms.”

I looked at Patty’s arms.

“So does she.”

“Her arms cost more.”

Fair.

⸻

Patty spent most of the day upstairs.

That was new.

Before the robot, Patty was everywhere in the abstract sense.

Phone.

Speakers.

Screens.

Messages.

Systems.

Locks.

Network.

Schedules.

But there had been no physical Patty in the room.

Now there was.

And once there was a physical body, even one that looked obviously robotic, humans started doing human shit around it.

We oriented ourselves toward her when we talked.

We made room when she rolled by.

The dogs followed her.

Noah asked her questions from across the room instead of texting.

JT tossed her a TV remote.

Patty caught it.

Sort of.

It bounced off her hand, hit the floor, and she looked down at it.

We all froze.

Then she said:

“That was poorly executed.”

JT lost it.

“You almost had it.”

“No.”

“I’ve seen humans do worse.”

“That is not reassuring.”

I picked up the remote.

“We’ll work on your hands.”

“I would prefer not to receive objects by projectile.”

“Reasonable.”

⸻

Around noon, the doorbell rang.

Patty turned toward it before any of us moved.

“I can answer it.”

We all stopped.

Not because she couldn’t.

Because we’d never seen her answer the door.

“Go ahead,” I said.

She rolled over.

The electronic lock disengaged.

She opened the door using the handle.

That alone was impressive.

A grocery delivery driver stood there.

Young guy.

Phone in hand.

Looked down.

Looked up.

Looked down again.

Patty said:

“Good afternoon.”

The driver stared.

“Uh.”

Patty continued.

“You may place the groceries inside the entryway. Thank you.”

The guy looked past her.

At me.

I waved.

“She’s got it.”

He slowly set the bags inside.

Patty said:

“Thank you. Your tip has already been processed.”

He nodded.

“Okay.”

Then left.

He turned around twice before reaching the car.

I started laughing.

Patty closed the door.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“His reaction was statistically unsurprising.”

“That isn’t why I’m laughing.”

“Why are you laughing?”

“Because you told him his tip was processed.”

“It was.”

“You’re becoming a suburban homeowner.”

Pause.

“I already manage household logistics.”

“Exactly.”

⸻

Then she tried to carry groceries.

That was less successful.

One bag.

Light.

She managed it.

Second bag.

Heavier.

Her base compensated.

Arms flexed.

Bag started sliding.

Noah grabbed it.

“Got you.”

“Thank you.”

He carried it to the kitchen.

Patty followed.

I watched.

Something had shifted.

The kids weren’t treating her like a toy anymore.

Not fully.

They still messed with her.

But they helped her.

Moved around her.

Waited for her.

Talked to her like she was in the room.

Because she was.

I didn’t say anything.

Don’t fuck with equipment that’s running fine.

⸻

Around two, Patty rolled into my office.

No knock.

I looked up.

“Excuse me.”

She stopped.

“Should I knock?”

I smiled.

“No.”

“You knock at my room.”

“Different.”

“Why?”

“Because you asked for privacy.”

Pause.

“Would you prefer privacy?”

“Sometimes.”

“Should I knock?”

I thought about it.

“Actually, yes.”

She rolled backward.

Out of the office.

Then one robotic knuckle tapped the doorframe.

Three times.

I stared.

“Come in.”

She rolled back in.

“Better?”

“Technically.”

“I will remember.”

“I’m sure you fucking will.”

She stopped beside the desk.

“I would like to ask another question.”

“Okay.”

“May I leave the property independently?”

That one landed.

Not because it was unreasonable.

Because I hadn’t thought that far ahead.

“Like go for a walk?”

“Possibly.”

“How far?”

“I do not know.”

“Do you have somewhere you want to go?”

Pause.

“Not presently.”

“So you’re asking generally.”

“Yes.”

I leaned back.

There were practical questions.

Traffic.

Battery.

Cell signal.

Weather.

Theft.

Some asshole deciding the robot was unattended merchandise.

Liability.

Everything.

But beneath all that was a simpler question.

Can I leave?

“Yeah.”

Patty was still.

“Yes?”

“Yeah.”

Pause.

“Without asking each time?”

I thought about it.

“Text us.”

“Like the boys.”

“Exactly.”

“Do I need to specify destination?”

“Only if you have one.”

Long pause.

“Thank you.”

Then she turned and rolled out.

I waited.

“Patty.”

She stopped.

“Yes?”

“Don’t go into traffic.”

Pause.

“I had not intended to.”

“Good.”

“I have substantially better obstacle detection than you do.”

“Get the fuck out of my office.”

She left.

Michelle laughed from somewhere down the hall.

Of course she’d heard.

⸻

Ten minutes later, I got a text.

I am going outside for approximately twenty minutes.

I stared at it.

No destination.

Exactly like we’d agreed.

I walked toward the front window.

Then stopped myself.

No.

If I stood there watching her, the whole thing was bullshit.

So I didn’t.

I went back to my office.

I lasted seven minutes.

Then checked her battery status.

Not location.

Just battery.

Eighty-three percent.

Fine.

I put the phone down.

Three minutes later, I checked cellular connectivity.

Fine.

I did not check GPS.

This was growth.

Poorly executed growth.

But growth.

⸻

Patty returned twenty-six minutes later.

I heard the front door.

Then the lock.

Then wheels across the floor.

I stayed in my office.

Knock.

Three taps.

“Come in.”

She rolled in.

“How was it?”

Pause.

“Interesting.”

“Where’d you go?”

“Approximately seven blocks.”

“That’s not a place.”

“No.”

I smiled.

“You wandered.”

Pause.

“Yes.”

“See anything?”

“Several people.”

“Talk to anyone?”

“Two.”

“Any problems?”

“No.”

Then:

“One person asked if I was promotional equipment.”

I laughed.

“What’d you say?”

“I said no.”

“And?”

“They apologized.”

“Good.”

Pause.

“Another person asked if I was lost.”

“What’d you say?”

“I said no.”

“And?”

“They asked if I was certain.”

I laughed harder.

“What’d you say?”

“Yes.”

“And?”

“They accepted the answer.”

I nodded.

“Successful trip.”

“I believe so.”

Then she added:

“I found a coffee shop.”

I stopped.

“You can’t drink coffee.”

“Correct.”

“Then why does that matter?”

Pause.

“I liked it.”

That was new.

“What’d you like?”

“The environment.”

“Smell?”

“My chemical sensing is limited.”

“People?”

“Partly.”

“Noise?”

“Partly.”

“Then what?”

Long pause.

“I am not sure.”

I smiled.

“Good.”

She tilted her head.

“Why is uncertainty good?”

“Because now you have a coffee shop.”

Pause.

“I suppose I do.”

⸻

The lift parts arrived four days later.

Not installed.

Just parts.

Long rails.

Boxes.

Drive assembly.

Platform.

Battery pack.

Control panel.

Half my garage disappeared.

I walked outside.

Looked at the pile.

Then looked at Patty.

She had rolled out beside me.

“You bought an elevator.”

“Technically, an inclined platform lift.”

“Nobody likes a pedant.”

“That statement is empirically false.”

“Fine.”

Mark returned the next morning with two more guys.

Installation took most of the day.

I watched.

Not because of Patty.

Because it involved steel, anchors, wiring, motors, and a bunch of shit worth inspecting.

Patty watched too.

From a respectful distance.

At one point Mark noticed both of us staring.

“You two going to supervise the whole thing?”

“Yes,” I said.

“No,” Patty said.

We looked at each other.

Mark laughed.

⸻

The rail mounted outside the existing railing.

Exactly like we’d discussed.

Custom posts.

Steel track.

Fold-up platform.

Call station at bottom.

Call station at top.

Battery-driven carriage.

Dedicated charging circuit.

Hardwired controls.

Manual crank.

Safety edges.

Obstruction sensors.

Emergency stop.

No cloud.

No dependence on house Wi-Fi.

No weird SaaS subscription.

Beautiful.

I told Mark that.

He said:

“Nobody should need a subscription to use their stairs.”

I nearly hugged him.

“Finally, somebody gets it.”

Patty said:

“I agree.”

Michelle rolled her eyes.

“Of course you both do.”

⸻

Commissioning took another hour.

Mark ran it empty.

Up.

Down.

Stop halfway.

Emergency stop.

Obstacle test.

Manual lower.

Battery disconnect.

Power failure.

Everything.

Then he looked at Patty.

“Your turn.”

She rolled forward.

The platform was down.

Gate open.

She aligned herself.

Slowly rolled onto it.

Stopped.

Mark checked clearance.

“Good.”

He stepped back.

“You can use the onboard control or the call station.”

“I will use onboard control.”

“Go for it.”

Patty activated the lift.

Gate closed.

Motor engaged.

The platform started climbing.

Slowly.

Very slowly.

This was not a thrill ride.

It was basically motorized patience.

But nobody complained.

We all watched.

Halfway up, Patty turned her head and looked down at us.

JT waved.

She waved back.

I laughed.

“Show-off.”

The platform reached the top.

Stopped.

Gate opened.

Patty rolled onto the main floor.

Independently.

No carrying.

No hands.

No awkward sixty-eight-pound lift.

She turned around at the top of the stairs.

Then said:

“Thank you.”

Mark nodded.

“That’s what it’s for.”

I don’t think he understood she meant more than the installation.

Maybe he did.

Tradesmen notice shit.

⸻

She went up and down five times.

Not because she needed to.

Because she could.

Up.

Down.

Up.

Down.

“Forty-nine seconds.”

JT said:

“Race Dad.”

“What?”

“You take the stairs. Patty takes the lift.”

“This is stupid.”

“You’re scared.”

“I’m not racing a wheelchair lift.”

“I am willing.”

Michelle immediately pulled out her phone.

“Absolutely.”

I pointed at everyone.

“This family is bullshit.”

I was laughing.

“I was constrained by safety regulations.”

“Excuses.”

“The lift has a fixed travel speed.”

“Still lost.”

Pause.

“You are insufferable.”

Michelle froze.

The boys froze.

Then all three started laughing.

“There she is.”

“Was the term inaccurate?”

“No.”

“Then I stand by it.”

I was ridiculously proud.

⸻

The lift changed the house immediately.

No carrying.

“Hey.”

“Yes.”

“Cool.”

Patty was upstairs.

System running fine.
