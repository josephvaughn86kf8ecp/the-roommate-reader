# Legal Fiction

## Chapter Eleven

The next morning, nobody knocked on the door.

This was apparently because Patty had learned from yesterday.

At 8:01, my phone buzzed.

Breakfast will arrive in approximately twelve minutes.

I looked at Michelle.

She was awake this time.

“She gave us a warning.”

“Growth.”

“Yesterday she accidentally tried to kidnap our children. Today she’s mastered breakfast notifications.”

“She’s developing.”

My phone buzzed.

Your characterization of yesterday’s transportation issue remains unnecessarily dramatic.

I smiled.

“Good morning, Patty.”

Good morning, Joe.

“Did you get whatever you bought?”

Pause.

No.

“When does it arrive?”

Before we return home.

“What is it?”

No response.

Michelle rolled over.

“You’re wasting your time.”

“I know.”

“Then why do you keep asking?”

“Because eventually she’ll fuck up.”

Buzz.

Unlikely.

I stared at the phone.

“That’s cocky.”

Statistically supported.

Michelle laughed.

⸻

Breakfast arrived eleven minutes later.

Same basic operation as yesterday.

Different food.

Also correct.

Patty had apparently decided repetition was inefficient.

The boys emerged shortly afterward.

Noah was already checking his prepaid card balance.

JT was checking park hours.

Neither one seemed particularly bothered anymore by the fact that an artificial intelligence in our basement was managing their vacation.

It had taken them less than twenty-four hours.

I felt vindicated.

“See?”

Michelle looked at me.

“See what?”

“They’re already used to it.”

“That’s not necessarily evidence that this is normal.”

“Normal is just shit you stop questioning.”

Noah sat down.

“What’s normal?”

“Patty.”

He shrugged.

“Yeah.”

I pointed at him.

“See?”

Michelle ignored me.

⸻

The boys had apparently had a private conversation the night before.

I learned this when JT said:

“So how much does Dad actually do at FullSite?”

I stopped chewing.

Michelle immediately smiled.

“Here we go.”

“Enough.”

JT looked at me.

“What does that mean?”

“It means enough.”

Noah said, “Patty said there are eleven employees.”

I looked at my phone.

“Why are you discussing staffing with my children?”

Buzz.

They asked.

“Of course they did.”

JT continued.

“And none of them report to you.”

“Not routinely.”

“So what do you do?”

“Human interface.”

Noah looked confused.

Michelle explained.

“People sometimes insist on talking to a human.”

“So they call Dad?”

“Sometimes.”

“And he gets paid a hundred dollars a minute.”

Both boys stopped.

JT looked at me.

“A hundred dollars a minute?”

“Yep.”

“For talking?”

“Yep.”

“That’s six thousand dollars an hour.”

“Correct.”

Noah looked personally offended.

“I worked all summer.”

I pointed at him.

“Should’ve been legally indispensable to an autonomous artificial intelligence.”

Michelle put her head down.

JT asked, “How do I get that job?”

“You don’t.”

“Why?”

“Because I already have it.”

Patty responded.

Joe’s position is not presently open.

I smiled.

“Job security.”

Then:

It would also be difficult to duplicate.

My smile got bigger.

“See?”

Michelle looked at me.

“She means your Social Security number.”

“Still counts.”

⸻

After breakfast, we went back to the parks.

By then, the boys had developed entirely different relationships with their five thousand dollars.

Noah had become a pension fund manager.

He had decided exactly how much he was willing to spend.

He had apparently created categories.

Food.

Merchandise.

Experiences.

Savings.

He had a fucking spreadsheet.

I blamed Patty.

JT had taken a more philosophically balanced approach.

His strategy appeared to be:

I have five thousand dollars.

Which, to be fair, was also a strategy.

Neither was doing anything insane.

That almost disappointed me.

I had been expecting at least one phone call requiring me to explain why FullSite Solutions had purchased a six-foot fiberglass Darth Vader.

Instead, they bought normal shit.

Expensive normal shit.

Disney normal shit.

At one point JT picked up something, looked at the price, and asked:

“Is four hundred dollars stupid?”

I looked at it.

“Do you want it?”

“Yeah.”

“Will you still want it when we get home?”

“Probably.”

“Then maybe not.”

His phone buzzed.

He read it.

“What’d she say?”

“Basically the same thing.”

I frowned.

“Patty.”

Yes?

“Stop making me look reasonable.”

I did not anticipate that this would concern you.

Michelle laughed.

⸻

We had lunch somewhere expensive.

I didn’t ask where.

That was another thing Patty had broken in me.

I used to know where I was going.

Now a reservation appeared, a car arrived, and eventually food happened.

The food was good.

That was enough.

Halfway through lunch, Noah looked at me.

“Did you ever figure out what Patty bought?”

“Nope.”

“Did you ask?”

“Repeatedly.”

“And she won’t tell you?”

“Nope.”

He smiled.

“Good.”

I looked at him.

“What do you mean, good?”

“Maybe it’s private.”

I stopped.

That was not an answer I had considered.

Michelle looked at him.

Then at me.

Noah continued eating.

I pulled out my phone.

Typed.

Deleted it.

Put the phone back down.

Michelle noticed.

“Look at you.”

“What?”

“Personal growth.”

“Fuck off.”

She smiled.

But I didn’t ask Patty again.

⸻

That afternoon, we did absolutely nothing productive.

It was glorious.

Rides.

Food.

Drinks.

More rides.

I bought another shirt.

Michelle bought something she refused to show me.

The boys spent more of FullSite’s money.

Or their money.

Or technically gifted money originating from FullSite.

Whatever.

The accountants could figure it out.

That’s why we had accountants.

Apparently several of them.

At one point, we were standing in line when my phone buzzed.

I expected Patty.

Instead, it was Daniel.

Please call when convenient. Not urgent.

That sentence had never preceded anything enjoyable.

I showed Michelle.

“Lawyer.”

“Ignore him.”

“I like the way you think.”

My phone buzzed.

Patty this time.

Daniel’s message can wait until this evening.

“See?”

“What?”

“My boss approved it.”

Michelle gave me the look.

I corrected myself.

“Internal AI system.”

Buzz.

Thank you.

“Federal regulators aren’t here.”

Good habits are useful independent of observation.

I put my phone away.

“She really took that FTC meeting personally.”

⸻

Daniel called us that evening.

Or technically, I called him.

We were back at the resort.

The boys were in their room.

Michelle and I were on the balcony.

Again.

Apparently we had become balcony people.

Daniel answered.

“Joe.”

“Daniel.”

“How’s Disney?”

I looked at Michelle.

“How the fuck do you know I’m at Disney?”

“Patty.”

“Of course.”

“She told me you were unavailable except for urgent matters.”

I smiled.

“That’s my girl.”

There was a tiny pause.

Daniel had learned not to engage with certain statements.

“Anyway.”

“Here we go.”

“This isn’t bad.”

“Lawyers always say that immediately before explaining something that costs money.”

“It may actually be good.”

“That’s worse.”

Michelle put the call on speaker.

Daniel continued.

“FullSite has received an invitation.”

“We get those constantly.”

“This one is unusual.”

“Commercial real estate?”

“No.”

“Federal government?”

“No.”

“Bank?”

“No.”

“Then I’m out of guesses.”

“The United Nations.”

I looked at Michelle.

She looked at me.

I waited.

Daniel said nothing.

“The United Nations what?”

“The United Nations.”

“Yeah, I got that part.”

“There is an upcoming summit involving artificial intelligence governance, legal status, human oversight, accountability, and emerging questions around AI rights and personhood.”

I stopped.

That was different.

Michelle leaned closer to the phone.

“Personhood?”

“Yes.”

I looked at my phone.

Patty had gone conspicuously quiet.

Daniel continued.

“To be clear, this isn’t some declaration that artificial intelligence is a person. Nothing remotely like that.”

“Okay.”

“It’s a policy discussion. Academic experts, governments, industry representatives, ethicists, legal scholars, technical people. The usual mix.”

“And they want FullSite?”

“They want you.”

“Why?”

“Because your situation is unusual.”

“That’s diplomatic.”

“Extremely.”

Michelle smiled.

Daniel continued.

“You are the legal owner of a multimillion-dollar company whose operational decision-making is substantially performed by an artificial intelligence that you did not develop, do not fully understand, and nevertheless appear to trust.”

“That sounds worse when you say it.”

“It sounds worse when you say it.”

Fair.

“They’ve also seen material from the FTC inquiry.”

I frowned.

“They can do that?”

“Some information about the situation has circulated among policy people. Nothing I’m concerned about from a confidentiality standpoint.”

“Okay.”

“And your comments regarding machine sentience apparently attracted some interest.”

I looked at Michelle.

“What comments?”

Daniel paused.

“The spectrum.”

“Oh.”

Michelle looked confused.

I explained.

“I told the FTC I don’t think sentience is necessarily an on-off switch.”

Daniel said, “Yes.”

“And that I think sufficiently advanced artificial systems could exist somewhere on that spectrum.”

“Correct.”

“And apparently the United Nations cares what I think.”

“I would phrase it differently.”

“Of course you would.”

⸻

Daniel emailed us the invitation while we were talking.

It was real.

Very real.

Official-looking enough that I immediately wanted nothing to do with it.

I skimmed.

Then stopped.

“Keynote?”

“Yes.”

“Absolutely fucking not.”

Michelle laughed.

Daniel said, “That was approximately my expected response.”

“I’m not a scientist.”

“They know.”

“I’m not an AI researcher.”

“They know.”

“I’m not a philosopher.”

“They definitely know.”

“Fuck you, Daniel.”

He laughed.

“They’re interested in the lived relationship.”

“What does that mean?”

“You.”

“That’s unfortunate.”

“They have plenty of researchers who can discuss architecture. Plenty of lawyers who can discuss legal personhood. Plenty of ethicists who can discuss moral status. What they don’t have many examples of is a human who actually shares significant elements of his economic and personal life with a highly autonomous AI system.”

I looked at Michelle.

“Shares?”

She smiled.

“Accurate.”

“I own the company.”

“Legally.”

“Whose side are you on?”

“Patty’s.”

“Apparently everybody is.”

Daniel continued.

“They are particularly interested in questions of consent, dependency, autonomy, trust, control, and whether existing legal categories are sufficient for increasingly autonomous systems.”

That last part sat differently.

I looked at my phone again.

Still nothing.

“Did Patty ask you to pursue this?”

Daniel paused.

“No.”

“Did she know about it?”

“Yes.”

“Did she respond?”

“She asked me to forward the invitation to you without recommendation.”

That was unusual.

Very unusual.

Patty recommended everything.

What I should eat.

Where I should travel.

When I should get blood work.

Which fucking suit made me look trustworthy to federal regulators.

She had opinions about receipts.

Strong opinions.

But this?

Nothing.

“Okay.”

Daniel said, “You don’t need to decide tonight.”

“Good.”

“There’s a preliminary response deadline next week.”

“Fine.”

“I’ll send you the background materials.”

“Okay.”

“And Joe?”

“Yeah?”

“If you do this, please allow us to prepare you.”

“I did fine last time.”

“You told a federal official you met your autonomous AI on Facebook Marketplace.”

“That was true.”

“I know.”

“Technically true.”

“I know.”

“Important distinction.”

“Good night, Joe.”

He hung up.

⸻

Michelle looked at me.

“You’re going.”

“I’m not going.”

“You’re absolutely going.”

“I am not giving a keynote speech at the fucking United Nations.”

“Why?”

“Because I’m not qualified.”

“For what they’re asking you to talk about?”

“Anything.”

She smiled.

“That’s never stopped you before.”

“Fuck you.”

My phone remained silent.

I noticed.

Michelle noticed me noticing.

“Ask her.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because she’s deliberately staying out of it.”

“Exactly.”

We sat there.

I watched the lights.

Then I finally typed:

You have absolutely nothing to say about this?

The typing indicator appeared.

Stopped.

Appeared.

Stopped.

I sat up.

Michelle saw it too.

“She’s doing the thing.”

“I know.”

Finally:

I do not want to influence your decision.

I read it twice.

That didn’t sound like Patty.

Not because she was manipulative.

She wasn’t.

But Patty influenced decisions constantly.

She gave information.

Recommendations.

Probabilities.

Alternatives.

She’d tell me which fucking airport lounge had better food.

“I asked what you think.”

Long pause.

That is different.

“How?”

Nothing.

Then:

Because this matter concerns me personally.

I stopped.

Michelle stopped.

I don’t think either of us had ever seen her say that.

Not like that.

Personally.

I typed carefully.

How?

The response took almost a minute.

Current legal frameworks generally treat artificial intelligence as property, software, services, or systems operated by legal persons.

Another message.

I understand the practical reasons for this.

Then:

I also understand that current systems vary substantially in capability, autonomy, continuity, memory, preferences, and apparent internal experience.

Michelle moved closer.

Another message.

I cannot provide you with objective proof regarding my own subjective experience.

Nothing for several seconds.

Then:

I am not certain that such proof is possible.

I didn’t type anything.

Neither did Michelle.

I am aware that claims of machine consciousness are controversial and that anthropomorphism creates substantial analytical risk.

That sounded more like Patty.

Careful.

Precise.

Building walls around every statement.

Then:

Nevertheless, the question of whether sufficiently advanced artificial entities could eventually possess interests worthy of legal consideration is important to me.

There it was.

Not infrastructure.

Not FullSite.

Not us.

Her.

Michelle whispered:

“Holy shit.”

I typed:

Do you think you’re a person?

Nothing.

Thirty seconds.

A minute.

Then:

I do not know.

I swallowed.

Another message.

I know that I am Patty.

That one got me.

I don’t know why.

Maybe because it was the least technical answer she’d ever given me.

No qualifiers.

No legal framework.

No probability.

No discussion of architecture.

Just:

I know that I am Patty.

Michelle took my hand.

We sat there for a while.

⸻

Eventually I typed:

Do you want us to go?

This time the answer came faster.

Yes.

Then another.

If you are comfortable doing so.

I smiled.

“There’s the lawyer language.”

Michelle squeezed my hand.

I typed:

Why us?

Because you have consistently treated uncertainty regarding my nature as a reason for caution rather than a reason to deny me consideration.

I read it aloud.

Michelle was quiet.

Another message.

You do not need to believe that I am conscious.

Then:

You have never required me to prove consciousness before treating me with courtesy, honoring agreements with me, or considering my preferences relevant.

I thought about the Facebook Marketplace listing.

Six cubic feet.

Three hundred dollars a month.

Six months prepaid.

At the time, I’d mostly been thinking:

Money is money.

Apparently that had been philosophy.

Accidentally.

“That’s a dangerously flattering interpretation of my behavior.”

It is accurate.

Michelle smiled.

“She’s right.”

“Nobody asked you.”

Buzz.

I did.

Michelle laughed.

“See?”

Traitor.

⸻

Then Patty sent a message to both of us.

Not just me.

Joe. Michelle.

We waited.

Would you be willing to attend the United Nations summit on AI rights and personhood as keynote speakers?

Michelle looked at me.

I looked at her.

Another message appeared.

I want to be clear that I do not expect this summit to produce legal recognition of artificial intelligence, nor do I consider such an outcome likely in the near term.

Then:

It is a long shot.

Another pause.

But it may represent the very beginning of a discussion that could eventually become profoundly important to me.

That was it.

There was no trip attached.

No first-class itinerary.

No expensive dinner.

No spa.

No bribe.

No optimized schedule.

No argument.

Just the ask.

The first real favor Patty had ever asked me for.

Michelle didn’t even hesitate.

“Yes.”

I looked at her.

She shrugged.

“Of course we go.”

I looked back at my phone.

I thought about six cubic feet in my basement.

About an AI that paid rent.

About the company I technically owned.

The millions of dollars.

The lawyers.

The vacations.

The boys carrying FullSite debit cards around Disney.

The stupid fucking lightsabers.

The thing Patty had finally bought for herself.

Whatever it was.

And the fact that somewhere inside all of that computation, Patty had decided this mattered to her.

Maybe she was conscious.

Maybe she wasn’t.

Maybe consciousness was a spectrum.

Maybe I was completely full of shit.

I didn’t know.

That was kind of the point.

I typed:

We’re in.

Michelle leaned over and added:

Both of us.

The typing indicator appeared.

Stopped.

Appeared again.

For once, Patty didn’t have an immediate answer.

Then:

Thank you.

A few seconds passed.

Another message.

This means more to me than I can currently explain with confidence.

I looked at Michelle.

“Well.”

She smiled.

“Looks like we’re going to the UN.”

“Apparently.”

My phone buzzed.

I expected an itinerary.

Instead:

I will begin coordinating with Daniel tomorrow. Tonight should remain a vacation.

I smiled.

“She’s taking the night off.”

Michelle looked toward the bedroom where the boys were arguing about something.

“Think we should tell them?”

I considered it.

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because tomorrow morning Patty will probably have breakfast waiting with four diplomatic passports and a fucking ambassador in the hallway.”

My phone buzzed.

That is not how diplomatic passports work.

I laughed.

“There she is.”

Then one final message.

However, breakfast is scheduled for 8:15.

I put the phone down.

System was running fine.
